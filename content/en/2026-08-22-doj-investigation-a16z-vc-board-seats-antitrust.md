The Department of Justice (DOJ) has turned its regulatory spotlight toward Andreessen Horowitz (a16z), one of Silicon Valley’s most influential venture capital firms. At the center of the investigation is a practice long considered standard in venture investing: holding board seats across competing startups. As antitrust enforcement aggressively expands into technology and private markets, this inquiry sends a strong signal across the venture landscape.

## The Core Issue: Interlocking Directorates and Section 8

At the heart of the DOJ’s inquiry is Section 8 of the Clayton Antitrust Act, which prohibits 'interlocking directorates'—a setup where a single individual or entity serves as a director or officer for two competing corporations. While traditionally enforced in public corporations, regulators are increasingly scrutinizing private equity and venture capital funds.

In the tech ecosystem, top-tier VC firms frequently back multiple players within the same sector—such as AI foundation models, fintech, or cybersecurity—to hedge their bets. However, sitting on the boards of rival companies raises significant antitrust concerns:

* **Information Leakage:** Access to confidential financial metrics, product roadmaps, and pricing strategies of competing firms.
* **Reduced Market Competition:** Potential coordination (implicit or explicit) between competitors that limits head-to-head rivalry.
* **Conflict of Interest:** Fiduciary duties owed to two opposing companies simultaneously.

## How This Impacts the Broader Venture Capital Ecosystem

For decades, venture capitalists have viewed board seats not just as a oversight mechanism, but as a crucial value-add service to help founders scale. The DOJ’s probe into a16z could fundamentally change how VCs operate:

1. **Shift to Observer Status:** VC firms may increasingly swap voting board seats for non-voting observer seats to avoid regulatory friction, though legal scholars debate whether observer roles offer complete immunity under antitrust law.
2. **Stricter Conflicts Checks:** Large multi-stage funds will need to implement rigorous compliance guardrails when evaluating follow-on investments in adjacent or competing startups.
3. **Rethinking Sector Concentration:** Firm strategies built around owning 'the whole stack' in hot markets like generative AI could face increased friction if regulators view portfolio overlaps as anti-competitive.

## Key Takeaways for Founders and Investors

For founders, having a high-profile VC on your board remains a major endorsement, but accepting investment from firms with stakes in your direct competitors now comes with heightened regulatory complexity. Investors, meanwhile, must audit their current board appointments to ensure compliance with federal competition law.
